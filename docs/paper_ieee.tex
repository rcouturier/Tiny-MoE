\begin{abstract}
Small Mixture-of-Experts language models built with an always-on shared expert appear to
under-use their routed experts: removing the entire routed branch costs little in perplexity,
while removing the single shared expert costs a great deal. We show that this asymmetry reflects
optimization dynamics and architectural proportion, not the computation being performed. Across
models of 58M to 436M parameters the imbalance is transient: the 134M reference model equalizes
its two branches by roughly 300M tokens, while a twice-deeper model stays shared-dominated longer.
The cause is a gradient-exposure race that the shared expert, updated on every token, wins against
routed experts updated on a $k/E$ fraction of them. The race is controllable: slowing the shared
expert reallocates computation to the routed branch at no cost in perplexity, and the reallocation
persists even when the handicap covers only the first tenth of training. Where the branches settle
is governed jointly by their relative width and by routing granularity, not by the number of
experts. At fixed routing an eightfold change in the shared expert's width moves its share of
ablation impact from 0.28 to 0.81 at essentially unchanged perplexity; at identical widths, top-1
and top-4 routing land at 0.97 and 0.17.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers replace a dense feed-forward block with a router that dispatches
each token to a small number of experts. The DeepSeekMoE design~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe} adds an
always-on \emph{shared} expert beside the \emph{routed} ones, on the intuition that it absorbs
computation common to all tokens and thereby frees the routed experts to specialize. Small MoEs
are inexpensive to train and every token can be labelled, which has made them a common testbed for
studying how experts divide work.

In this regime a consistent observation recurs. Ablating the entire routed branch barely moves
held-out perplexity, whereas ablating the lone shared expert degrades it severely. Taken at face
value this indicates that the routed experts are nearly redundant, and it admits two explanations
that both attribute the effect to the architecture: over-parametrization of the routed branch, or
suppression by the shared expert.

Neither explanation is supported by our measurements, and the imbalance is largely uninformative
about what the computation requires. It is the joint product of two things with no obvious
connection to the task: how quickly each branch is trained, and how wide each branch is. The
apparent redundancy is transient, disappearing once the model has seen a few hundred million
tokens. Its cause is a competition for gradient signal that the shared expert wins by
construction, and that a single learning-rate multiplier transfers to the routed branch at no cost
in perplexity; applying it over the first tenth of training suffices, and the allocation persists
long after the handicap is lifted. Where the branches come to rest is set jointly by their
relative width and by the routing granularity, not, as the mechanism naively suggests, by the
number of routed experts: holding the routed branch identical down to its parameter shapes and
varying only the shared expert's width moves the shared branch's share of ablation impact from
0.28 to 0.81, while at identical widths top-1 and top-4 routing land at 0.97 and 0.17. Granularity
is the only one of these levers with a measurable cost, and single-expert routing is its
degenerate limit.

The question is data-centric as much as architectural. What the routed experts are supposed to
acquire is structure present in the training corpus, and we measure that directly: every token
carries the label of the corpus it was drawn from, so expert assignment can be scored against data
domain rather than inferred from inspection. The diagnostic error we describe is a measurement
error about data-driven structure, made at the wrong point in training.

Two senses of redundancy must be distinguished: a branch is ablation-redundant if removing it is
cheap, functionally redundant if the other could have carried its work. Our results separate them.
With enough training the routed experts are not ablation-redundant at all, while the branches are
close to interchangeable in where the computation may reside. The split is therefore a largely
free design choice: among configurations of comparable size, perplexity varies by less than 0.1
while the branch split spans 0.25 to 0.81. This freedom is relevant whenever the routed experts
are themselves the object of study, as in interpretability, expert pruning and modular
editing~\cite{muennighoff2024olmoe,lu2024notall}, and it means that a diagnosis of expert
redundancy drawn from an undertrained checkpoint is misleading rather than merely imprecise. The
choice of lever then matters: widening the routed branch relative to the shared one more than
doubles domain specialization, whereas the learning-rate control does not reliably move it.

\section{Related Work}

Conditional computation through a sparsely gated MoE layer was introduced by Shazeer et
al.~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously} and scaled by GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard} and Switch
Transformer~\cite{fedus2022switch}, which popularized the auxiliary load-balancing loss in the
form we use; ST-MoE~\cite{zoph2022stmoe} added the router $z$-loss for stability. Our reference
configuration is top-2, in the style of Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}.

The always-on shared expert originates with DeepSeekMoE~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe} and carries
through DeepSeek-V2 and V3~\cite{deepseekv2,deepseekv3} and open families such as
Qwen2~\cite{qwen2technical}, on the argument that isolating common knowledge in a shared branch
lets the routed experts specialize more sharply. DeepSeekMoE pairs it with a second argument, that
finer expert segmentation sharpens specialization, of which Section~\ref{sec:topk} tests a
controlled analogue in isolation. Both concern what the branches represent; our results concern
how they are trained and proportioned, and suggest that at small scale the latter dominates.
Observational studies report that experts organize more by token identity and surface form than by
topic~\cite{jiang2024mixtral,muennighoff2024olmoe}, and Wang et al.~\cite{wang2026myth} argue that
routing reflects hidden-state geometry rather than domain expertise; these characterize training's
outcome, while we add a causal probe and a dynamical explanation. Mouzouni~\cite{mouzouni2026phases}
finds, across the checkpoints of large open MoEs, that routing passes through distinct phases, so
that what an early checkpoint shows about load balance is not what training ends with; we reach
the parallel conclusion for branch importance. Our mechanism instantiates a broader phenomenon, the early
phase of training committing a network to structure that later training refines rather than
revisits~\cite{achille2019critical,frankle2020early}, a phase whose routing instability motivated
StableMoE~\cite{dai2022stablemoe} and whose expert divergence has recent
theory~\cite{chen2022understanding}.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model} A decoder-only MoE in native PyTorch, built on the open-source Tiny-MoE
implementation~\cite{ebied2026tinymoe}, with Multi-head Latent Attention~\cite{deepseekv2}, RoPE~\cite{su2021roformer} and RMSNorm~\cite{zhang2019rmsnorm}. Each
layer carries $E$ routed experts and one shared expert with top-$k$ routing. The reference
configuration has $E=8$, $k=2$, 8 layers, hidden size 512, context 512 and 134M parameters; every
expert, routed or shared, has intermediate width 1024. Section~\ref{sec:ratio} varies the two
widths independently, writing them $I$ per routed expert and $I_{\mathrm{shared}}$ for the shared
one; configurations range from 58M to 436M parameters. All runs use AdamW ($\beta_1{=}0.9$,
$\beta_2{=}0.95$, decay 0.1), cosine to zero after 1500 warmup steps, batch 64 at length 512, peak learning rate (LR) $3\times10^{-4}$, load-balancing coefficient $10^{-2}$, router $z$-loss $10^{-3}$,
bfloat16, capacity factor 1.25 in training and none in analysis; 30\,000 steps is 983M tokens, on
one RTX A6000.

\paragraph{Data} Pre-training streams three token-labelled corpora:
FineWeb-Edu~\cite{penedo2024fineweb} (60\%), Cosmopedia-v2~\cite{benallal2024cosmopedia} (25\%)
and OpenWebMath~\cite{paster2023openwebmath} (15\%), tokenized with a 32k vocabulary and packed to
512 tokens. Held-out evaluation uses a skip-offset of the same streams rather than a disjoint
split, so absolute perplexities may be optimistic through near-duplication; every claim below rests on
differences between conditions evaluated on the identical stream, not on levels.

\paragraph{Measuring where the computation lives} Perplexity (PPL) is the exponential of the mean
token-level cross-entropy on the held-out stream, $\mathrm{PPL} = \exp(-N^{-1}\sum_t \log p(x_t
\mid x_{<t}))$, so at the values reported here a difference of 0.1 PPL is about 0.03 nats per
token. We ablate a whole branch, either all routed experts or the shared expert, and record the
resulting increase in PPL; single-expert ablation is uninformative under top-$k$ routing, since
the router reroutes to the survivors. We
summarize the two measurements by the shared branch's share of total ablation impact,
$s = \Delta_{\text{shared}}/(\Delta_{\text{shared}} + \Delta_{\text{routed}})$, so that $s=0.5$
denotes branches of equal importance and $s\rightarrow1$ a shared-dominated model. Almost every
trajectory dips to a minimum and then rises slowly, the exception being the top-1 configurations
of Section~\ref{sec:topk}, which decline monotonically; we therefore report the minimum reached
over the trajectory and, where the endpoint matters, the value at 983M tokens. Every configuration
in Tables~\ref{tab:lr}, \ref{tab:contrast} and~\ref{tab:topk} has at least two seeds, four for
the reference, which covers top-1 and both extremes of the width ratio, where the trajectories
behave least alike; across them the seed standard deviation on $s$ is at most 0.03, and under
0.01 at top-1, an order of magnitude below every effect discussed here. Single-seed runs are
identified where they appear. We report perplexity and ablation impact rather than downstream
accuracy because at this scale standard benchmarks sit near chance and would not resolve the
differences studied here; the question is where the computation lives, not what it is worth on a task.
As a reference point, a dense feed-forward network at the reference model's active width (58M parameters) reaches perplexity 3.11 at 983M tokens against the reference MoE's
3.00, and a variant trained without the shared expert reaches 3.03: the architecture justifies its inactive parameters, while the always-on branch contributes roughly in proportion to its parameter share, its distinctive contribution being organizational (Section~\ref{sec:spec}).

\paragraph{Whether $s$ measures computation or output norm} Zero-ablation perturbs the residual
stream by an amount that scales with the removed branch's output norm, and both levers studied
below modulate that norm. Table~\ref{tab:valid} validates the statistic. Substituting each
ablated branch's per-layer mean output for zeros reproduces $s$ within 0.07 everywhere, ordering
unchanged. The norm share dissociates from $s$ in both directions: under top-1 the routed branch
holds 24\% of the norm and 3\% of the impact, and under a slowed shared expert the shared branch
holds 32\% of the norm and 10\% of the impact. A fixed scalar of 2 or 0.5 on the shared output
moves the split by at most 0.07 at flat perplexity, the network \emph{absorbing} it by rescaling
weights until the trained norm share returns within 0.01 of baseline, whereas width manipulations,
which no rescaling absorbs, move $s$ by 0.32. Contribution norm follows function here; it does not
set it.

\section{Token-Dependence of Branch Importance}
\label{sec:obs}

We train the reference model for 983M tokens with four seeds and measure branch importance at
every checkpoint (Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}a). At 98M tokens removing the shared expert costs
$+3.44\pm0.17$ perplexity against $+0.82\pm0.03$ for the whole routed branch, so the shared expert
accounts for 81\% of the ablation impact and the routed experts appear close to redundant. This
is the regime in which many pilot studies operate, including our own earlier experiments. By 295M
tokens the branches have equalized ($+1.99$ against $+1.98$, $s=0.50$) and they remain so through
983M ($+2.45\pm0.18$ against $+2.48\pm0.11$), while perplexity falls from 4.18 to 3.00. Routed
specialization grows in parallel, the expert--domain normalized mutual information (NMI, defined
in Section~\ref{sec:spec}) rising from 0.0008 at initialization to 0.0174.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig1_trajectory.pdf}
\caption{(a) Ablation impact of removing the shared expert against all eight routed experts, four

\label{fig:traj}

Depth delays the crossing, in the direction that makes the phenomenon harder to detect rather than
easier (Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}b). A 16-layer, 251M-parameter model never crosses within our budget:
it reaches a minimum of $s=0.54$ near 491M tokens and drifts back to 0.59 by 983M. That baseline
is a single run, though the sweep replicate of Section~\ref{sec:mech} tracks it to within 0.02
over their common range. More residual capacity allows the shared expert's initial advantage to
compound further, so the larger the model, the longer an observer may incorrectly conclude that
its routed experts are redundant.

The implication is narrow but robust. Branch importance is not a property of the architecture
that can be measured once; it is a function of how much data the model has seen. Conclusions about
routed redundancy drawn below a few hundred million tokens do not survive further training, and
the effect is large enough to change the sign of a comparison.

\section{Mechanism: A Gradient-Exposure Race}
\label{sec:mech}

The asymmetry originates in the update rule rather than in the trained weights. Writing the block output on token $t$ as $y_t = f_{\mathrm{sh}}(x_t) +
\sum_{e\in\mathrm{topk}(t)} g_{t,e}\, f_e(x_t)$, with $f_{\mathrm{sh}}$ the shared expert, $f_e$
the routed ones and $g_{t,e}$ the router's gate weight, a batch of $N$ tokens delivers gradient
from all $N$ of them to the shared parameters but from only about $Nk/E$ to any given routed
expert. Under a common learning rate the shared expert therefore accumulates roughly $E/k$ times
more effective updates per parameter during the early phase, when gradients are large and the
allocation of work is still plastic. The residual stream compounds this effect by routing common computation to whichever submodule reduces the loss fastest. The account predicts that slowing the
shared expert should let the routed experts capture computation earlier, and that the crossover
should scale with $E/k$. The first holds; Section~\ref{sec:ratio} is devoted to what replaces the
second.

\subsection{Learning-rate handicap on the shared expert}

We place the shared expert's parameters in their own optimizer group at $\mathrm{lr}\times m$ and
leave everything else untouched. Table~\ref{tab:lr} reports the state at 98M tokens, deep in the
regime where the baseline looks shared-dominated. Slowing the shared expert makes the routed
branch load-bearing far earlier: a fivefold increase in routed ablation impact at a fixed token
budget, shifting the crossover from roughly 295M tokens to below 200M at $m=0.3$ and to before
training settles at $m=0.1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:lr}a). The effect is monotone and dose-dependent, and
it refutes the hypothesis that routed experts mature on an intrinsic schedule: their updates are
dominated by those of the shared expert.

These runs use a 15\,000-step schedule; at 98M tokens, where Table~\ref{tab:lr} is read, the
schedules' learning rates differ by under three percent, so the contrast is the multiplier. (The
baseline row is the reference run over two seeds, hence its last-digit difference from the
four-seed reading above.) Perplexity is unchanged across conditions and marginally lower when the
shared expert is slowed, so the shared expert is not necessary for the computation it performs in
the baseline. The allocation reflects the rate at which each branch learns, not a requirement of
the computation.

Interpolating Table~\ref{tab:lr} in $\log m$, the branches would balance at 98M tokens near
$m\approx0.26$, and near 0.21 in the 16-layer sweep below, close to the $k/E=0.25$ at which
per-parameter exposure is equal. We note the coincidence without leaning on it: it is a two-point
interpolation at a single $k/E$, it holds at one budget only, since by 295M tokens the
unhandicapped model balances by itself, and the response is not monotone in exposure.
Accelerating the shared expert instead, at $m=3$ over the full schedule, also lowers $s$, to
\SBOOST{} at 983M tokens with perplexity \PBOOST{} (two seeds), so the race can be lost by
converging too early as well as by learning too slowly. The handicap is the direction we
characterize.

A mechanism specific to one depth would be of limited interest, so we repeat the
sweep at 16 layers, where it reproduces almost exactly (Fig.~\ref{fig:lr}b): the shared share at
98M tokens falls from 0.82 to 0.64 to 0.21 across the same three multipliers, against 0.80, 0.56
and 0.14 at 8 layers, and perplexity is again unchanged. The deeper baseline reads 0.82 in this
sweep against 0.84 in the long-schedule run of Section~\ref{sec:obs}, within the seed spread.

\subsection{Persistence of a temporary handicap}

If the split is decided by an early race, a handicap covering only that race should suffice, and
the allocation should not revert once it is lifted. We test this at 16 layers by ramping $m$
linearly from 0.1 back to 1.0 by a chosen step, after which every parameter trains at the base
rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig3_hysteresis.pdf}
\caption{(a) Shared branch share at 16 layers for four handicap durations, the tick marking where
each handicap was lifted. (b) The resulting dose-response, with the never-lifted limit dashed.
Every ramped condition ends within 0.01 perplexity of the others.}
\label{fig:dose}
\end{figure}

The reallocation is not reversed. Once the handicap is removed the share stays near where the
handicap left it rather than returning to the baseline trajectory, and the shared branch reasserts
itself only slowly. The response is monotone in duration: the share at 983M tokens is 0.33, 0.26,
0.21 and 0.19 for handicaps lifted at 98M, 197M, 295M and 491M tokens, against 0.59 with no
handicap and 0.14 for a handicap never lifted.

Three features of that curve matter more than its monotonicity. A brief handicap already produces
most of the effect: lifting it after 98M tokens, one tenth of the budget, reaches 0.33, more than
half of the distance from the baseline to the never-lifted limit. The increments then shrink
(0.07, 0.05, 0.02), yet the never-lifted limit sits a further 0.05 below the longest ramp, so the
response flattens without plateauing in our range. Releasing the handicap is also marginally
preferable to keeping it: final perplexities are 2.88, 2.88, 2.88 and 2.89 for the four ramps,
against 2.90 for the baseline and 2.91 when the handicap is never released, the only condition
worse than no intervention. The multiplier therefore acts as a schedule intervention rather than a
permanent penalty, as a race-then-commit dynamic predicts, since the branch that wins the early
competition is the one the residual stream subsequently builds upon.

We replicated the 295M endpoint with a second seed and obtained the same summary statistics, a
minimum of 0.14 and 0.21 at 983M tokens. The other four conditions are single runs at one depth
and one handicap strength, so we report the shape of the dependence rather than a functional law.

\section{Determinants of the Resting Split}
\label{sec:ratio}

The mechanism explains why the shared expert leads and shows that the lead can be reversed. It
does not say where the split comes to rest, and the natural prediction that the resting point
follows $E/k$ is not borne out. We assemble twelve 8-layer configurations at top-2 routing: the
six runs of Table~\ref{tab:contrast}, a capacity-matched sweep over expert count ($E\cdot I =
8192$, $E\in\{4,8,16,32\}$), and three from an earlier ratio-1.0 sweep ($E = 4$, 16, 32 at
$I=I_{\mathrm{shared}}=1024$, 83M--436M parameters; the $E=4$ run stopped at 491M with its share
flat from 295M on, the rest reached 983M).

What the two contrasts share is the ratio $I/I_{\mathrm{shared}}$. Grouping every configuration by
that ratio yields bands that do not overlap (Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio}a): shared shares of 0.72--0.81
at ratio 0.25, 0.63--0.70 at ratio 0.5, 0.47--0.56 at ratio 1.0 and 0.25--0.37 at ratio 2.0. The
ratio-0.5 band is reached from both sides, by narrowing the routed experts at $E=16$ and by
widening the shared one at $E=8$, and lies 0.07 above the ratio-1.0 band, more than twice the seed
spread. Its upper end comes within 0.02 of the ratio-0.25 band, a margin the seed spread does not
resolve; the two are told apart only at fixed expert count, where the four $E=8$ ratios order
cleanly at 0.28, 0.47, 0.70 and 0.81. The ordering survives an eightfold range in ratio, an eightfold range in expert
count and a sevenfold range in parameter count.

Expert count neither explains the residual spread within a band nor acts consistently: at ratio
2.0 more experts raise the share (0.25 at $E=4$ to 0.37 at $E=8$), at ratio 0.25 they lower it
(0.79 at $E=8$ to 0.72 at $E=32$). Nor is this a capacity confound: holding $E\cdot I = 8192$
equalizes parameter counts, and perplexity is then flat across $E\in\{4,8,16,32\}$ at 2.99, 3.00,
3.00 and 3.02 while the share tracks the ratio that manipulation necessarily varies. Expert count
is close to a free parameter for perplexity, and moves the split only within a band's residual
spread.

\subsection{Effect of routing granularity}
\label{sec:topk}

claim to matter. We test it by holding the widths at $I = I_{\mathrm{shared}} = 1024$, so that the
width ratio is 1.0 throughout, and varying only $k$.

Table~\ref{tab:topk} shows that $k$ enters, and that it is the strongest single lever in this
study: moving from top-1 to top-4 at identical widths swings the split from 0.97 to 0.17, further
than the 0.53 obtained by an eightfold change in the shared expert's width. The width-ratio result must
therefore be read as holding at fixed routing. This refines rather than refutes the $E/k$
prediction of Section~\ref{sec:mech}: it fails on the $E$ axis, where varying $E$ from 4 to 32 at
a fixed width ratio leaves the split within the single band 0.47--0.56 and not even in a
consistent direction, but succeeds on the $k$ axis and in the predicted direction. What fails is
the \emph{ratio} $k/E$; what survives is $k$ alone. Counted per branch rather than per parameter,
the routed branch receives $k$ expert passes per token whatever $E$, against one for the shared
branch, which matches the direction observed; weighting those passes by width, however, yields the
active-width share $\phi$ introduced below, which two runs refute, so the count organizes the $k$
axis without becoming a law.

Unlike the width ratio, this lever incurs a cost. Top-1 costs 0.13 in perplexity and top-4 doubles
the routed floating-point cost per token, whereas the width manipulations moved the split across
0.25 to 0.81 within a 0.07 band of perplexity.

A natural repair would fold $k$ into a single scalar, the shared branch's share of \emph{active}
feed-forward width $\phi = I_{\mathrm{shared}}/(I_{\mathrm{shared}} + kI)$. Two runs refute it.
Both hold $k\cdot I = 2048$ and $I_{\mathrm{shared}} = 1024$, giving the same $\phi = 0.33$ as the
reference ($s_{\min} = 0.47$), and vary only how that width is delivered, as four experts of 512
or one of 2048. Both should land near 0.47; neither does. Four-of-512 reaches 0.29 (perplexity
3.06 at 96M parameters), so granularity acts on its own even among $k\geq2$ configurations: the
same active width delivered through more, narrower experts engages the routed branch more. At
matched active width and a fixed expert pool this is a controlled test of DeepSeekMoE's
fine-grained segmentation argument~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe}, and it comes out in that argument's
favour, on engagement here and on specialization in Section~\ref{sec:spec}. One-of-2048 stays at
0.95, so top-1's collapse is not a width effect either; doubling the per-token routed width
changes nothing when the router cannot mix experts. This behaviour is expected: unable to blend
experts, the routed branch never becomes functionally useful, its ablation costs 0.29 perplexity
and its domain NMI \emph{declines} toward zero over training, consistent with the known difficulty
of single-expert routing~\cite{fedus2022switch}.

The data therefore support a joint statement, not a scalar law: at fixed granularity the width
proportion orders every configuration we measured; at fixed active width, finer granularity lowers
the shared share further; and single-expert routing never engages the routed branch at any width
we tested. These results compose with the mechanism. The gradient race decides which branch
arrives first, the branch proportion and routing granularity where the allocation settles once it
is over. The width manipulations are reparametrizations rather than levers on quality, moving the
shared share from 0.25 to 0.81 across the eight 127M--234M configurations at perplexity 2.95 to
3.02.

\section{Reallocation and Routed Specialization}
\label{sec:spec}

The motivation for wanting routed experts engaged is usually that they are the object of study, so
it matters whether experts forced to carry more computation also organize their work more sharply.
We measure gate-weighted normalized mutual information between expert assignment and the domain
label of each token, comparing at matched training steps and, where possible, at identical routed
architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:spec}). Shuffling the domain labels over the same tokens gives a
permutation null below $10^{-4}$ wherever we computed it, so the values below, small in absolute
terms, sit two orders of magnitude above chance, top-1 excepted. The result is not uniform across the two levers.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig5_specialization.pdf}
\caption{Routed domain specialization against branch split, engagement increasing to the right;
the width series is read at 983M tokens, the learning-rate series at 491M.}
\label{fig:spec}
\end{figure}

The width lever is effective. Holding $E=8$ and $I=1024$ so that the routed branch is identical
across runs and varying only $I_{\mathrm{shared}}$, domain NMI at 983M tokens rises from 0.0103 to
0.0174 to 0.0226 as the shared share falls from 0.82 to 0.50 to 0.34 (values at 983M tokens, not
the trajectory minima of Table~\ref{tab:contrast}): a factor of 2.2 in specialization obtained
without touching the routed experts at all, and without cost in perplexity. The granularity runs
point the same way, their routed NMI rising from 0.0003 at top-1 to 0.0235 at top-4, and to
0.0301, the highest measured anywhere in this study, when the same active width is delivered as
four experts of width 512. Nor is the gain specific to the domain axis: across the same width
trio, surface-form NMI rises from 0.118 to 0.141 and frequency NMI from 0.039 to 0.056, with
position at the control floor throughout, though the headline factor of 2.2 is domain-specific and
the surface-form gain a more modest 1.2. In absolute terms surface form, four lexical classes
against three domains, carries six to twelve times more routing information than domain: the
experts organize first by form, as the observational literature reports~\cite{jiang2024mixtral,muennighoff2024olmoe} and as a
geometric account
of routing predicts~\cite{wang2026myth}. The data-centric claim is accordingly not that the routed
experts are domain experts, but that how much domain structure they carry is set by the design. Deleting the shared expert makes the same point from the
other side: routed domain NMI falls to 0.0113 without it, so the always-on branch, whose absence
costs only 0.03 perplexity when the model is trained without it, increases rather than decreases
the organization of the routed experts.

The learning-rate lever does not, or not reliably. At 16 layers, measured at 491M tokens where the
baseline remains shared-dominated, slowing the shared expert raises NMI monotonically, 0.0108 to
0.0135 to 0.0151, all three points single runs from the same sweep. At 8 layers, where balance has
already been reached by then, the response is non-monotone, 0.0191, 0.0195 and 0.0175 for $m =
1.0$, 0.3 and 0.1, ending below the baseline; this series reads the long-schedule reference
against short-schedule sweep runs, so we conclude from it only that the gain is not reliable. The
dose-response series is similarly equivocal: NMI increases with handicap duration among the ramped
runs, but the unhandicapped baseline falls inside their range rather than below it.

What these disagreements share is that specialization tracks how much the routed branch has
genuinely been trained, which rises as it takes over computation but flattens near balance and can
fall once the shared branch is starved past it. Engagement and specialization are therefore
related but distinct axes, and the distinction is practical: experiments requiring more
interpretable routed experts should vary the branch widths rather than the learning rate, even
though both move the split by comparable amounts. We exclude cross-$E$ comparisons because NMI
grows mechanically with expert count, and every value is measured at balancing coefficient
$10^{-2}$, which suppresses domain NMI, plausibly by the suppression of shared hidden-state
directions that Wang et al.\ attribute to the balancing loss~\cite{wang2026myth}, so magnitudes are conditional on that setting though each
contrast is internal to it.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

A small MoE's division of feed-forward labour is only weakly determined by the task. Two
quantities a practitioner sets freely, the branches' relative learning rate and relative width,
move the split across almost its whole attainable range while leaving perplexity within 0.1; a
third, routing granularity, moves it further at a cost the first two do not carry. The shared
expert does not perform computation the routed experts could not; it performs the computation it
acquired first, and retains as much of it as its width allows.

Three consequences follow. The first is diagnostic: expert redundancy cannot be read off a single
early checkpoint, since 98M and 983M tokens disagree and deeper models stay misleading longer, so
branch importance belongs in a trajectory. The second concerns design: our own early sweep over
expert count read as a dependence on $E$ only because capacity and parameter count rode along with
it, and sweeps should fix what they are not testing. The third is prescriptive. A modest
learning-rate handicap engages the routed branch much sooner at no measured cost and need only be
applied early, but engagement bought that way does not reliably come with sharper specialization:
if the routed experts are wanted merely \emph{used}, either lever serves; if they are wanted
\emph{legible}, the width ratio is the one that works. The mechanism also predicts for routing
families we did not test, since expert-choice routing~\cite{zhou2022expertchoice} equalizes token
counts across routed experts but leaves the shared expert's every-token exposure intact, so the
race and its remedy should survive it.

For data-centric practice the consequence is concrete. Expert routing is increasingly used as a
handle on the training corpus itself: attributing behaviour to data domains, pruning experts that
serve rare slices, or editing a model by replacing the experts associated with a subset of the
data. Each use assumes that routed experts carry data-domain structure, and that the assumption
can be checked by ablation. Our measurements show that the check is valid only once the branches
have equalized, and that the amount of domain structure the routed experts carry is itself a
design variable: the same corpus, the same token budget and the same routed parameters give a
2.2-fold range in expert--domain mutual information according to the width ratio alone. A pipeline
that treats expert--data alignment as a fixed property of a trained model will mis-attribute in
proportion to how early it looks and how the branches were sized.

Several limits bound these claims, and concern generality rather than internal validity. Every
model is a DeepSeek-style MoE with a single shared expert, between 58M and 436M parameters, trained
for at most 983M tokens on one data mixture and one hardware configuration. What is established is
the internal account at that scale; whether the crossover, the controls and the width ordering
transfer to models of billions of parameters, to other shared-expert layouts and to other corpora
is open, and is the first thing we intend to test. The width-ratio result is an ordering over an
eightfold range at one depth, not a functional form, and no single scalar we tried subsumes width
and routing at once. The dose-response is replicated at one endpoint, its other points resting on
a single seed each. Finally, our evidence is perplexity, ablation impact and routing statistics:
at this scale standard benchmarks are near chance, so whether a reallocated split changes
downstream behaviour, reasoning or the quality of generated answers, which is what a practitioner
would ultimately want, needs a larger model to answer.

Routed experts in small Mixture-of-Experts models are therefore not redundant in the ablation
sense: they are out-competed early by a shared expert that receives gradient from every token.
At the scale we studied, where such a model puts its feed-forward computation is, to a good
approximation, something the designer chooses rather than something the model discovers.

\section*{Reproducibility}

The model, the training code and the analysis toolkit, including whole-branch ablation and routing
capture, are available at \url{https://github.com/rcouturier/Tiny-MoE} with the run configurations and the per-checkpoint
