\title{Routed Experts Are Not Redundant:\\ Training Dynamics and Branch Proportion in\\ Small Mixture-of-Experts Models}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{AUTHOR NAMES}
\IEEEauthorblockA{AFFILIATION\\ email@domain}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Small Mixture-of-Experts language models built with an always-on shared expert appear to
under-use their routed experts: removing the entire routed branch costs little perplexity, while
removing the single shared expert costs a great deal. We show that this asymmetry reflects
optimization dynamics and architectural proportion rather than any property of the computation
being performed. Training Mixture-of-Experts models of 58M to 251M parameters on a domain-labelled
corpus, we find the imbalance is transient, the two branches equalizing by roughly 300M tokens,
and we trace it to a gradient-exposure race in which the shared expert, updated on every token,
out-competes routed experts updated on a $k/E$ fraction of them. The race is causally
controllable: slowing the shared expert reallocates computation to the routed branch, and a
handicap applied over only the first tenth of training is enough to make that reallocation
permanent. Where the branches finally settle is governed not by the number of experts but by the
ratio of their widths, which orders eleven configurations spanning eightfold in ratio and
eightfold in expert count. Across that entire range held-out perplexity varies by less than 0.1,
so the placement of feed-forward computation in a small Mixture-of-Experts model is close to a
free design choice.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
mixture of experts, training dynamics, sparse models, language models, expert specialization
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers replace a dense feed-forward block with a router that dispatches
each token to a small number of experts. The DeepSeekMoE design~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe} adds an
always-on \emph{shared} expert beside the \emph{routed} ones, on the intuition that the shared
expert absorbs computation common to all tokens and thereby frees the routed experts to
specialize. Because small MoEs are inexpensive to train and every token can be labelled, they have
become a common testbed for studying how experts divide work.

In this small-scale regime a striking observation recurs. Ablating the entire routed branch barely
moves held-out perplexity, whereas ablating the lone shared expert degrades it severely. Read at
face value, the measurement says the routed experts are nearly useless, and it invites two
explanations: either small MoEs are over-parametrized on the routed side, or the shared expert
actively suppresses the routed ones. Both explanations attribute the imbalance to the
architecture.

We argue that neither is right, and that the imbalance says almost nothing about what the
computation requires. It is instead the joint product of two things that have no obvious
connection to the task: how quickly each branch is trained, and how wide each branch is. The
apparent redundancy is transient, disappearing once the model has seen a few hundred million
tokens. Its cause is a race for gradient signal that the shared expert wins by construction, and
that a single learning-rate multiplier settles in either direction at no cost in perplexity.
Applying that multiplier over the first tenth of training suffices; once the race is decided, the
allocation persists after the handicap is removed. Finally, where the two branches come to rest is
set by the ratio between their widths, and not, as the mechanism naively suggests, by the number
of routed experts. Holding the routed branch identical down to its parameter shapes and varying
only the shared expert's width moves the shared branch's share of ablation impact from 0.28 to
0.81.

Taken together these results reframe the shared/routed split as a near-neutral reparametrization.
Held-out perplexity varies by less than 0.1 across configurations whose branch split spans 0.25 to
0.81, which means a practitioner may place the computation more or less where they wish. That
freedom matters whenever the routed experts are themselves the object of interest, as they are in
interpretability work, expert pruning, and modular editing, and it means that a diagnosis of
expert redundancy drawn from an undertrained checkpoint is not merely imprecise but misleading.

\section{Related Work}

Conditional computation through a sparsely gated MoE layer was introduced by Shazeer et
al.~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously} and scaled by GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard} and Switch
Transformer~\cite{fedus2022switch}, the latter establishing the auxiliary load-balancing loss that
we also use; ST-MoE~\cite{zoph2022stmoe} added the router $z$-loss for stability. Our backbone is a
top-2 configuration in the style of Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}.

The always-on shared expert originates with DeepSeekMoE~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe} and carries
through DeepSeek-V2 and V3~\cite{deepseekv2,deepseekv3}, motivated by the argument that isolating
common knowledge in a shared branch lets the routed experts specialize more sharply. That argument
concerns what the branches represent. Our results concern how the branches are trained and
proportioned, and they suggest that at small scale the second consideration dominates the first.

Observational studies of expert behaviour report that experts organize more by token identity and
surface form than by topic, both in Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral} and across training in
OLMoE~\cite{muennighoff2024olmoe}. These studies characterize the outcome of training. We add a
controlled causal probe and identify a training-dynamics cause for the small-scale imbalance that
observational analysis alone cannot isolate.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model} A decoder-only MoE implemented from scratch in PyTorch, using Multi-head Latent
Attention~\cite{deepseekv2}, RoPE~\cite{su2021roformer} and RMSNorm~\cite{zhang2019rmsnorm}. Each
layer carries $E$ routed experts and one shared expert with top-$k$ routing. The reference
configuration has $E=8$, $k=2$, 8 layers, hidden size 512, context 512 and 134M parameters; every
expert, routed or shared, has intermediate width 1024. Section~\ref{sec:ratio} varies the two
widths independently and writes them $I$ per routed expert and $I_{\mathrm{shared}}$ for the shared
one. Configurations range from 58M to 251M parameters. All runs use batch 64, sequence length 512,
learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ with 1500 warmup steps, and an auxiliary load-balancing coefficient
of $10^{-2}$; 30\,000 steps correspond to 983M tokens. Every experiment fits on one RTX A6000.

\paragraph{Data} Pre-training streams three token-labelled corpora: FineWeb-Edu~\cite{penedo2024fineweb}
(60\%), Cosmopedia-v2~\cite{benallal2024cosmopedia} (25\%) and OpenWebMath~\cite{paster2023openwebmath}
(15\%), tokenized with a 32k vocabulary and packed to 512 tokens. Held-out evaluation uses a
skip-offset of the same streams rather than a fully disjoint split.

\paragraph{Measuring where the computation lives} We ablate a whole branch, either all routed
experts or the shared expert, and record the resulting increase in held-out perplexity. Ablating a
single expert is uninformative under top-$k$ routing because the router simply reallocates its
tokens to the survivors; only whole-branch ablation isolates a branch's contribution. We summarize
the two measurements by the shared branch's share of total ablation impact,
\begin{equation}
s = \frac{\Delta_{\text{shared}}}{\Delta_{\text{shared}} + \Delta_{\text{routed}}},
\end{equation}
so that $s=0.5$ denotes branches of equal importance and $s\rightarrow1$ a shared-dominated model.
Every trajectory we measured dips to a minimum and then rises slowly, so we report both the
minimum reached and the value at 983M tokens. Seed-to-seed variation on $s$ is approximately 0.02,
an order of magnitude below the effects discussed below.

At 98M tokens the shared expert accounts for 81\% of the ablation impact and the routed experts do
indeed look close to redundant. This is the regime in which many pilot studies operate, including
our own earlier experiments. By 295M tokens the branches have equalized, and they remain balanced
through 983M; routed specialization grows in parallel, with domain-conditioned normalized mutual
information rising from 0.0008 to 0.017. All four seeds follow the same trajectory with tight
error bars.

The practical reading is narrow but firm. Branch importance is not a property of the architecture
that can be measured once; it is a function of how much data the model has seen. Conclusions about
routed redundancy drawn below a few hundred million tokens do not survive further training, and
the effect is large enough that the sign of a comparison can change.

\section{A Gradient-Exposure Race}
\label{sec:mech}

Why should the shared expert lead at all? The asymmetry is present in the update rule before it is
present in the weights. Writing the block output on token $t$ as
\begin{equation}
y_t = s(x_t) + \sum_{e\in\mathrm{topk}(t)} g_{t,e}\, f_e(x_t),
\end{equation}
with $s$ the shared expert and $f_e$ the routed ones, a batch of $N$ tokens delivers gradient from
all $N$ of them to the shared parameters but from only about $Nk/E$ to any given routed expert.
Under a common learning rate the shared expert therefore accumulates roughly $E/k$ times more
effective updates per parameter during the early phase, when gradients are large and the
allocation of work is still plastic. The residual stream compounds this: it routes common
computation to whichever submodule reduces loss fastest, which further starves the routed experts
of useful gradient.

This account makes two predictions. Reducing the shared expert's learning rate should let the
routed experts capture computation earlier, and the crossover should scale with $E/k$. The first
holds; the second does not, and Section~\ref{sec:ratio} is devoted to what replaces it.

\subsection{The race can be rigged}

We place the shared expert's parameters in their own optimizer group at $\mathrm{lr}\times m$ and
leave everything else untouched. Table~\ref{tab:lr} reports the state at 98M tokens, deep in the
regime where the baseline looks shared-dominated.

\centering

\label{tab:lr}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
$m$ & remove routed & remove shared & $s$ & PPL \\
\midrule
1.0 (baseline) & $+0.85$ & $+3.46$ & 0.80 & 4.16 \\
0.3            & $+1.94$ & $+2.48$ & 0.56 & 4.10 \\
0.1            & $+4.46$ & $+0.72$ & 0.14 & 4.10 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{Shared branch share against training tokens for three shared-expert learning-rate
multipliers, two seeds. Slowing the shared expert moves the crossover earlier, dose-dependently,

\label{fig:lr}

Slowing the shared expert makes the routed branch load-bearing far earlier: a fivefold increase in
routed ablation impact at a fixed token budget, shifting the crossover from roughly 295M tokens to
below 100M at $m=0.3$ and to before training settles at $m=0.1$. The effect is monotone and
dose-dependent. It refutes the hypothesis that routed experts simply mature on their own schedule;
they were being out-competed.

Held-out perplexity is unchanged across the three conditions, and if anything marginally better
when the shared expert is slowed. The shared expert is therefore not necessary for the computation
it performs in the baseline. The model places the work there because that is where it can be
learned fastest, not because that is where it belongs.

The control reproduces at twice the depth. On a 16-layer, 251M-parameter model the share at 98M
tokens falls from 0.82 to 0.64 to 0.21 across the same three multipliers, again at unchanged
perplexity. Depth does change the timing: the deeper baseline reaches a minimum of only 0.54 and
does not cross to balance within 983M tokens, ending at 0.59. Left alone, a deeper model stays in
the shared-dominated regime considerably longer, which sharpens rather than softens the warning of
Section~\ref{sec:obs}.

\subsection{A temporary handicap is enough}

If the split is decided by an early race, a handicap covering only that race should suffice, and
the allocation should not revert once it is lifted. We test this on the 16-layer model by ramping
$m$ linearly from 0.1 back to 1.0 by a chosen step, after which every parameter trains at the base
rate. Fig.~\ref{fig:dose} shows the outcome for four ramp endpoints.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig6_hysteresis.pdf}
\caption{Left: shared branch share against training tokens at 16 layers, for four handicap
durations; the tick marks the point at which each handicap was lifted. Right: the resulting
dose-response, final share against how long the handicap was held. Perplexity is flat across the
whole series.}
\label{fig:dose}
\end{figure*}

The reallocation does not undo. Once the handicap is removed the share stays near where the
handicap left it rather than returning to the baseline trajectory, and the shared branch re-asserts
itself only slowly. The response is monotone in the duration of the handicap: the share at 983M
tokens is 0.33, 0.26, 0.21 and 0.19 for handicaps lifted at 98M, 197M, 295M and 491M tokens
respectively, against 0.59 with no handicap at all. Final perplexities are 2.88, 2.88, 2.88 and
2.89 against the baseline's 2.90.

Two features of that curve matter more than its monotonicity. A brief handicap already does most
of the work: lifting it after 98M tokens, one tenth of the budget, still reaches 0.33, which is
about two thirds of the total reallocation available. And the curve saturates, the difference
between the 295M and 491M endpoints being 0.02, equal to the seed noise on this quantity. Those two
points are not distinguishable, and holding the handicap beyond roughly 295M tokens buys nothing we
can measure. The knob is thus a schedule intervention rather than a standing tax on the shared
expert, which is what a race-then-commit dynamic predicts: what wins the early race is what the
residual stream subsequently builds upon.

We replicated the 295M endpoint with a second seed and obtained identical values. The remaining
three endpoints are single-seed, and the sweep covers one depth and one handicap strength, so we
report the shape of the dependence rather than a functional law.

\section{The Width Ratio Sets Where the Split Lands}
\label{sec:ratio}

The mechanism explains why the shared expert leads and shows that the lead can be reversed. It does
not say where the split comes to rest, and the natural prediction from the gradient-exposure
argument, that the resting point should follow $E/k$, turns out to be wrong. Across eleven 8-layer
configurations trained to 983M tokens, expert count is nearly irrelevant and the ratio of the two
branches' widths orders every run.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Two disentangling contrasts, both at $E{=}8$, $k{=}2$. Values are the minimum shared share
reached over the trajectory.}
\label{tab:contrast}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\emph{Varying routed width}, $I_{\mathrm{shared}}=1024$}\\
$I$        & 256    & 1024   & 2048 \\
$s_{\min}$ & 0.79   & 0.47   & 0.37 \\
parameters & 58.2M  & 133.7M & 234.4M \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\emph{Varying shared width}, $I=1024$; routed branch identical}\\
$I_{\mathrm{shared}}$ & 512 & 1024 & 4096 \\
$s_{\min}$ & 0.28   & 0.47   & 0.81 \\
parameters & 127.4M & 133.7M & 171.5M \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:contrast} isolates the two candidates. Both contrasts hold $E=8$ and $k=2$ fixed, so
expert count, routing and per-expert gradient share are constant throughout. The second contrast is
the decisive one: with $I$ pinned at 1024 the routed branch is identical across the three runs, the
same number of experts of the same width receiving the same routed capacity $k\cdot I = 2048$, and
yet the split moves by 0.53. Whatever governs the resting point, it is not the absolute capacity of
the routed branch.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig3_ratio.pdf}
\caption{Left: minimum shared share against the branch width ratio, one point per configuration,
coloured by expert count; the bands at each ratio do not overlap. Right: the decisive contrast, in
which the routed branch is identical across all three runs and only the shared expert's width
changes.}
\label{fig:ratio}
\end{figure*}

What the two contrasts share is the ratio $I/I_{\mathrm{shared}}$. Grouping every configuration by
that ratio yields bands that do not overlap, as Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio} shows: shared shares of
0.72--0.81 at ratio 0.25, 0.63 at ratio 0.5, 0.47--0.56 at ratio 1.0 and 0.25--0.37 at ratio 2.0.
The ordering survives an eightfold range in ratio, an eightfold range in expert count and a
fourfold range in parameter count.

Expert count does not explain the residual spread within a band, and it does not even act in a
consistent direction: at ratio 2.0 more experts raise the share, from 0.25 at $E=4$ to 0.37 at
$E=8$, while at ratio 0.25 more experts lower it, from 0.79 at $E=8$ to 0.72 at $E=32$. A reader
might still suspect that the sweep confounds expert count with capacity, since a naive sweep over
$E$ at fixed per-expert width grows the routed branch as it grows $E$. Holding the product
$E\cdot I$ at 8192 removes that confound and equalizes parameter counts; perplexity is then flat
across $E\in\{4,8,16,32\}$ at 2.99, 3.00, 3.00 and 3.02, while the share continues to track the
ratio that the same manipulation necessarily varies. Expert count is close to a free parameter for
both quantities in this regime.

The two results compose rather than compete. The gradient race decides which branch arrives first;
the relative width of the branches decides how much computation each can absorb, and therefore
where the allocation settles once the race is over. Both are reparametrizations rather than levers
on quality: across the seven configurations between 127M and 234M parameters the minimum shared
share spans 0.25 to 0.81 while final perplexity spans only 2.95 to 3.02.

\section{Discussion}

The picture that emerges is that a small MoE's division of feed-forward labour is only weakly
determined by the task. Two quantities that a practitioner sets freely, the relative learning rate
of the two branches and their relative width, move the split across almost its entire attainable
range while leaving held-out perplexity within 0.1. The shared expert is not doing something the
routed experts could not do; it is doing what it got to first, and it keeps as much of the work as
its width allows.

Three consequences follow. The first is diagnostic. Expert redundancy cannot be assessed from a
single early checkpoint, because the measurement at 98M tokens and the measurement at 983M tokens
disagree, and deeper models remain in the misleading regime longer. Branch importance should be
reported as a trajectory. The second is a matter of experimental design: our own $E/k$ prediction
survived a sweep over expert count only because that sweep let routed capacity ride along with the
variable under study, and it failed as soon as capacity and parameter count were matched. Sweeps
over architecture should fix what they are not testing. The third is prescriptive. When routed
experts are the object of study, a modest learning-rate handicap on the shared expert engages them
much sooner at no measured cost, and it need only be applied over the early part of training; more
durably, one should simply avoid making the shared expert wide relative to the routed ones.

Several limits bound these claims, and they concern generality rather than internal validity. All
models here fall between 58M and 251M parameters and see at most 983M tokens, on a single data
mixture and a single hardware configuration; whether the crossover and the two controls persist at
production scale is untested and is the obvious next question. The width-ratio result establishes
an ordering over an eightfold range at one depth, not a functional form, and we deliberately do not
fit one. The dose-response over handicap duration is replicated at a single endpoint, and its
remaining points rest on one seed each; the effect sizes there exceed the measured seed noise by a
comfortable margin, but the shape of the saturation deserves a denser sweep. Finally, all of our
evidence is perplexity and ablation impact, so we cannot say whether a reallocated split changes
behaviour on downstream tasks, which is the measurement a practitioner would ultimately want.

\section{Conclusion}

Routed experts in small Mixture-of-Experts models are not redundant. They are out-competed early in
training by a shared expert that receives gradient from every token, and the outcome of that
competition is settled by two hyperparameters rather than by the demands of the data. Slowing the
shared expert reverses the result, permanently, even when applied only over the first tenth of
training; the relative width of the two branches then fixes where the split comes to rest, spanning
shared shares from 0.25 to 0.81 while perplexity moves by less than 0.1. Where a small
Mixture-of-Experts model puts its feed-forward computation is, to a good approximation, something
the designer chooses rather than something the model discovers.

\section*{Reproducibility}

The model, training code and analysis toolkit, including branch ablation, routing capture and the
trajectory drivers, are released with this paper; every experiment runs on a single GPU. Two
implementation defects found and fixed during the study, a missing causal mask in the absorbed
attention inference path and a broken CUDA reduction in the load-balancing loss, are documented in
the version history.
